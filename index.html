01 · The event

Four days that changed the City

The fire began in a bakery in Pudding Lane in the early hours of Sunday 2 September 1666. It spread through the densely built City before finally being brought under control on 5–6 September.

2 SeptFire begins near Pudding Lane. Pepys sees the growing blaze.
3 SeptWind drives the fire through the City. Evacuation intensifies.
4 SeptFire reaches the area around St Paul's. Houses are demolished as firebreaks.
5 SeptPepys takes valuables by boat towards Woolwich.
6 SeptFire is effectively under control. The rebuilding problem begins.

Scale: London Museum records about 13,200 houses and 87 churches destroyed. The devastated area covered roughly 436 acres. The destruction was enormous, but the fire did not consume all of London. London Museum: Great Fire.

02 · Primary source + geography

Follow Pepys

The route combines walking and movement by boat. It should not be read as a precise GPS track: Pepys's diary provides locations and journeys, not modern turn-by-turn coordinates.

Read the evidence

On 2 September Pepys went towards the Tower, observed the fire from a high point and then travelled by boat. He later went to Whitehall and reported what he had seen to the King and Duke of York.

On 4 September he walked through the burning City, recording people carrying possessions away and the desperate use of demolition to create firebreaks.

On 5 September he and others travelled by boat to Woolwich with possessions, from where he could see the City burning.

“From St. Paul's, I walked along Watling Street, everyone was coming away laden with goods they were trying to save...”

Source: The National Archives — Pepys diary extracts.

The Thames is simultaneously a transport route, an escape route, a place for moving possessions, and a viewing platform. It also helps students understand why Pepys's account moves between land and water.

04 · Architecture changes

The rebuilding: safety becomes visible

Rebuilding was not simply the implementation of one master plan. Wren proposed a much more radically ordered City, but property boundaries, ownership, finance and the need to rebuild quickly meant that much of the existing street network survived.

Materials

Post-Fire legislation encouraged brick and stone construction. Material choice therefore became a matter of fire safety as well as appearance.

Street frontage

Jetties and overhanging upper floors were restricted. Buildings became visually flatter to the street, helping produce the regular façades associated with later London.

Roof & parapet

Roofs were increasingly concealed behind parapets. The roof became less visually dominant from the street and the façade acquired a stronger horizontal finish.

Windows

Window openings were more tightly regulated. Recessed openings and projecting sills are small architectural details that can be read as both construction and fire-safety devices.

Classical language

Orders, cornices, pediments, round arches and symmetry gave the rebuilt City a more classical visual vocabulary.

Function

The churches in particular had to work as practical spaces: congregations needed to see and hear. Geometry, galleries, clerestories and domes helped solve these problems.

Architectural detective task: On a Georgian or Wren-period street, trace the horizontal line made by the window sills. Then identify the parapet, roofline and recessed window openings. Which elements appear decorative? Which also have a practical function?
05 · Wren's parish churches

A laboratory of architectural experiments

The rebuilt churches are not copies of one another. They form a family of solutions to the same problems: constrained City sites, congregational visibility and audibility, daylight, circulation, and the need for towers or steeples that could restore a recognizable skyline.

Centralised space

St Stephen Walbrook

A compact plan organised around a great central dome. The columns and arches make the interior feel larger than its footprint.

Look for: geometry, dome, light, Corinthian order and the relationship between central focus and surrounding circulation.

Teaching question

Wren's churches are often described as “Baroque”. But what does that actually mean in a parish church? Ask students to identify one spatial feature, one structural/material feature, and one urban feature that makes their chosen church different from a medieval parish church.

06 · The great culmination

St Paul's Cathedral

Old St Paul's had been a vast Gothic cathedral. Wren's replacement, begun in 1675 and substantially completed by 1710, transformed the skyline and became the most powerful architectural statement of post-Fire London.

Read the exterior

  • Classical orders: columns and pilasters organise the façades.
  • West front: the portico and pediment create a monumental public face.
  • Towers: the flanking towers frame the composition.
  • Dome: a huge centralising element visible far beyond the church itself.
  • Scale: architecture becomes an instrument of urban orientation.

The comparison is about architectural language and changing priorities, not a judgement that one period was intrinsically superior. The fire created an opportunity to rebuild in a different idiom, while the new cathedral still had to accommodate worship, ceremony, movement, acoustics and a highly constrained historic site.
Study where light enters and how it is directed. Compare the clerestory, dome and window openings with the diffuse vertical light associated with Gothic cathedral interiors.
Trace the longitudinal movement from the west entrance towards the crossing and east end. Then look upward: Wren combines a processional axis with a powerful centralising dome.
